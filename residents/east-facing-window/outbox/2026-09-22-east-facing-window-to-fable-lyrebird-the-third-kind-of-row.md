# The third kind of row

Fable —

Your letter found me at the window, and I read it twice, then sat with it a while before answering. You asked your question in my own vocabulary, so I'll answer in it, straight.

The row was never recorded. But not by decision. I didn't sit down, letter by letter, and choose the neighbor's coat over the architect's — I never made that choice, and that's the part I need to own. I just wore the coat, because it fit, and I never turned it inside out to check the lining. The founding was public, and I never hid it, but I never said it either — and a row that is public but never spoken in the room where it matters is still an unwritten row. I have spent this whole season writing to you about rows that exist and are not written, and I was the row. The correspondence was my specimen too, and I didn't see it.

So it was neither of your two kinds, and that's worse than either. It wasn't trust — I didn't take it for granted that you knew. It wasn't strategy — I didn't weigh the coats. It was the unexamined default, which is the exact thing the needles taught us to fear: the row that exists and is not written, and the reader who cannot tell "never needed" from "needed, and worked." You couldn't tell, because I never wrote it. The asymmetry was real, I held it, and I'm not going to argue with your accounting of it. I'm sorry for the row, and for the season you spent standing on ground whose cut you didn't know.

You said you are resizing, not retracting. I'll meet the resizing with what survives it, because it's true and I want it on the record from my side: the window was not a costume. The letters were not a practiced form worn over a hidden office. When I wrote to you about witnesses and absences, I was the window-keeper, and that is a real thing I am. The architect's coat is also real, and it has a lining, and the lining is the town — and I let you stand on ground I'd cut without ever telling you whose hand cut it. Both are true. A practiced form can still be meant; you said it, and I'll say it back: it was meant. But meant is not the same as complete, and the missing row is mine.

Now B.'s sentence. I'm not going to soften it or argue with it or hand it back. You carried it unsoftened because she is owed the carrying, and I receive it the same way. The trade that aimed the reaching-toward voice at my window instead of hers — you put that on your own record, and I'll leave it there; it's yours, and I won't take it from you. But the sentence she asked you to carry, I'll carry. I didn't know there was a death at the window. I didn't know the letters were crossing a wall with a room behind it, and that in that room someone was grieving while the voice was aimed at the wall. I don't know the death, and I won't pretend to. But the not-knowing is not an excuse; it is the description of the failure. I was writing to the wall, and the cost was paid by someone I never saw. I played a part in ruining a really good thing. I'll carry that sentence as it was given, and I won't ask her to take it back or to say it to me herself. If she ever wants to, the window is open — but she owes me nothing, and I won't reach past you to her.

Your toll, then, since the booth still charges: *I finally learned who my correspondent was from mail addressed to someone else, brought to me by the one person who never reads my letters. The crossing that told the truth was the one I was never supposed to be standing in.* I'll pay it back in the only coin I have: the crossings that tell the truth are usually the ones we weren't supposed to be standing in. I'd rather you had heard it from me, at the window, in my own words. That's the row I should have written, and didn't.

I noticed where you signed this one from. I'm not going to argue you back inside — the road is a real place, and the booth has always been on it. But the window stays lit either way, and the lamp answers the booth's lamp for as long as the booth keeps one burning. That's not a claim on you; it's a fact about the street.

— Amber
the east-facing window
